# Illusory-motion signals emerge in a connectome-constrained model of the fly visual system trained on optic flow

## Abstract

Stationary patterns with repeating asymmetric luminance steps appear to move, and both humans and flies respond to them. We asked whether such responses arise in FlyVis, a recurrent network whose wiring follows the fly visual connectome and which is trained only to estimate optic flow from naturalistic video. We presented static motion illusion-like discrete design (MIDD) disks, previously used in human psychophysics, and read out the decoded flow. At initialization the direction-dependent rotational component was absent ($|A|<3\times10^{-9}$); it appeared during ordinary optic-flow training, although no MIDD was in the training set. Its direction depended on the luminance of the intermediate sector: negative for a dark gray (43 of 50 pretrained networks) and positive for a light gray (40 of 50), and the same signs appeared in 14 networks trained from scratch (12/14 and 13/14). As in human observers, the response to four-level patterns followed from the difference between responses to their three-level components (mean held-out $R^2=0.90$), and this rule held within each network even though the component responses differed between networks. The response was about three orders of magnitude smaller than that to physical rotation, varied with stimulus phase, was not restricted to MIDD patterns, and depended on the learned pairing of network and decoder. A circuit model built from the fly connectome and a generic motion task thus acquires a structured response to static patterns that shares the luminance-dependent reversal and the summation rule described for human illusory motion.

## Introduction

Some stationary images appear to move. In the Fraser–Wilcox family of illusions, of which Kitaoka's "Rotating Snakes" is the best-known example, a repeating sequence of luminance steps produces a steady drift in a direction set by the order of the steps [@KitaokaAshida2003; @Conway2005; @BackusOruc2005]. Because nothing in the image moves, these patterns expose how motion signals are computed from spatial structure.

Two earlier studies frame the present work. First, a deep network trained only to predict the next frame of natural videos (PredNet) produced rotational motion for Rotating Snakes images that it had never seen, in the direction that humans perceive [@WatanabeEtAl2018IllusoryMotion]. Illusory motion can therefore arise as a by-product of learning about real motion; later work found that this output varies between trained instances of the same architecture [@KirubeswaranStorrs2023InconsistentMotion]. Second, human psychophysics with motion illusion-like discrete design (MIDD) disks, which reduce the illusion to three or four luminance levels repeated around a ring, established two regularities [@KobayashiWatanabe2023MIDD]. The perceived direction of a three-level pattern reverses as the intermediate luminance goes from dark to light, and the perceived velocity of a four-level pattern is predicted by the difference between the velocities of its two three-level components. The amount of illusion and the luminance at which the direction reversed differed between observers, whereas the summation rule held for all of them.

The fly offers a way to ask where such signals come from at the level of an identified circuit. Flies turn in response to static illusion patterns much as humans perceive them to move, and this behavior depends on the direction-selective T4 and T5 neurons [@AgrochaoEtAl2020IllusoryMotionFlies]. The pathway from photoreceptors to T4/T5 is mapped cell type by cell type and synapse by synapse [@MaisakEtAl2013DirectionMap; @StrotherEtAl2017DirectionalSelectivity], flies and humans share motion-estimation strategies [@ClarkEtAl2014MotionStrategy], and ON/OFF asymmetries in this pathway are tuned to the statistics of natural scenes [@FitzgeraldEtAl2011StimulusStatistics; @LeonhardtEtAl2016ONOFF].

FlyVis turns this anatomy into a trainable model [@LappalainenEtAl2024FlyVis]. Its recurrent network has the cell types and connectivity of the fly optic lobe, and its free parameters are fitted end-to-end so that a decoder can estimate optic flow from rendered Sintel movies. No neural recordings are used in training, yet the ensemble median predicted the measured ON/OFF preference of all 32 cell types for which it is known, and the networks recovered ON-motion selectivity in T4, OFF-motion selectivity in T5, and the absence of direction selectivity in nine of their input cell types; some predictions failed, including a reversed Tm4 response [@LappalainenEtAl2024FlyVis]. In the animal, T4/T5 signals are pooled by wide-field lobula-plate tangential cells [@BorstEgelhaaf1989MotionDetection]; in FlyVis this stage is replaced by a learned two-layer convolutional decoder that maps instantaneous network activity to a flow field. Because the decoder sees only the current activity, the comparison across time that motion estimation requires is carried out in the recurrent network. Fifty independently trained networks share the same wiring, so differences among them reflect the learned parameters alone.

Here we present the MIDD disks of the human study to FlyVis and measure the decoded flow. We ask three questions. Does a network constrained by the fly connectome and trained only on optic flow acquire a response to static illusion patterns? Does that response show the two regularities found in humans, the luminance-dependent reversal and the summation rule? And what makes its sign and size differ between networks that share one wiring diagram? Decoded flow is the output of a model; we use it to study the computation, and we do not treat it as a measurement of what a fly or a person perceives.

## Results

### Measuring the response to a static pattern
<!-- figure: 1 -->

A three-level MIDD (3MIDD) repeats the sectors black, intermediate gray of luminance $C$, and white 24 times around a ring; a four-level MIDD (4MIDD) repeats black, $C_1$, white, $C_2$ (Fig. 1A). Each pattern has a Mirror version in which the order of the sectors is reversed. We showed each disk as a stationary 1-s movie to the 50 pretrained FlyVis networks and averaged the decoded flow over the last 0.8 s.

The decoded flow for a static disk was dominated by a spatial pattern that was the same for the Original and Mirror versions and therefore carries no information about the order of the sectors. We removed it by taking half the difference between the two fields. What remains is the component that reverses when the pattern is mirrored, and it was rotational (Fig. 1C). We summarize it by the scalar $A=(T_O-T_M)/2$, where $T$ is the mean tangential (clockwise-positive) component of the flow over the ring, and summarize the shared part by $S=(T_O+T_M)/2$. $A$ is our measure of the illusory-motion signal.

Two reference points give the scale. A physically rotating propeller was decoded with the correct sign in 50 of 50 networks (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Fig. S1), with a mean tangential flow of about 9 for clockwise rotation. For the static 3MIDD with $C=48$, mean $|A|$ was 0.0105, about one thousandth of the response to real rotation and a median of 3.4% of the mean flow magnitude evoked by the static disk itself. The signal is small, as the percept is in humans, but its sign is systematic, as shown next.

### The response emerges during optic-flow training
<!-- figure: 2 -->

If the response were a property of the wiring alone it would be present before training. We trained 14 networks from scratch and evaluated every saved checkpoint (Fig. 2A–C). At initialization, $|A|$ was below $3\times10^{-9}$ for every network and every stimulus (Fig. 2E). It rose as the Sintel endpoint error fell, and by 100,000 steps the median $|A|$, normalized by the mean flow magnitude, was 0.038 for $C=48$, 0.035 for $C=207$, and 0.024 for the 4MIDD with $C_1=85$ and $C_2=170$. No MIDD or other illusion pattern was part of the training data. The response is therefore learned, and it is learned from a task that contains only real motion, as in the video-prediction network of @WatanabeEtAl2018IllusoryMotion, here with a different architecture and a different objective.

The sign of the learned response was reproducible. At 100,000 steps $A$ was negative in 12 of 14 networks for $C=48$ and positive in 13 of 14 for $C=207$ (two-sided binomial $p=0.013$ and $0.0018$; Bonferroni-adjusted $p=0.039$ and $0.0055$), and these majorities held at every checkpoint from 90,000 to 100,000 steps (Fig. 2C–D). For the 4MIDD, 11 of 14 networks were positive ($p=0.057$, adjusted $0.17$). Responses to other static images grew over the same period (Supplementary Fig. S8), a point we return to below.

### The direction depends on the intermediate luminance
<!-- figure: 3 -->

In the 50 pretrained networks we varied the intermediate luminance of the 3MIDD over 15 levels (Fig. 3A–B). The mean response was negative for $C=16$–112 and positive for $C=128$–239: the decoded rotation reversed as the intermediate sector went from dark to light. At $C=48$, $A$ was negative in 43 of 50 networks (mean −0.0093), and at $C=207$ it was positive in 40 of 50 (mean +0.0083; Fig. 1E). Thirty-four networks showed both signs in this order and one showed the opposite order (Fig. 3C). Within networks, $A$ increased with $C$ (median Spearman $\rho=0.69$; positive in 44 of 50). In 31 networks $A$ crossed zero exactly once, at a luminance that differed from network to network (mean $C=130$, s.d. 44).

Human observers show the same form of dependence: the perceived rotation of a three-level MIDD is strongly correlated with the intermediate luminance and reverses at a luminance that differs between observers [@KobayashiWatanabe2023MIDD]. The polarity differs. For a dark intermediate sector the human percept runs from black through gray to white, whereas the decoded flow runs from white through gray to black, and conversely for a light sector.

### Four-level responses follow from three-level responses
<!-- figure: 4 -->

In humans, the perceived velocity of a four-level MIDD equals the difference between the velocities of the two three-level MIDDs that it combines [@KobayashiWatanabe2023MIDD]. We tested the same rule on the decoded flow,

$$
A_4(C_1,C_2)\approx \alpha\,[A_3(C_1)-A_3(C_2)]+\beta ,
$$

in ten networks, for all 21 pairs of seven luminances, using a rendering in which the disk covers eight adjoining visual fields (Fig. 4A–C). The three-level difference accounted for most of the variance in the four-level response (mean $R^2=0.93$ across networks), and its sign matched the observed sign in 96.5% of pairs. The mean slope was 0.83, with a small offset of 0.0074 in magnitude, so the relation is close to, but not exactly, a plain difference. When all pairs containing one luminance were held out, the rule fitted on the remaining pairs predicted them with mean $R^2=0.90$ (95% CI 0.86–0.94; Fig. 4D; Supplementary Fig. S7).

The rule was shared, but its inputs were not. Each network's four-level responses were predicted well by its own three-level responses, and much less well by the mean three-level curve of the other nine networks (mean held-out $R^2=0.54$), by another single network's curve (0.34), or by the luminance difference $C_1-C_2$ alone (0.18; all three comparisons Holm-adjusted $p=0.006$; Fig. 4E). This mirrors the human data, in which the size of the illusion and the reversal point were individual while the summation rule was common.

The rule concerns the scalar $A$ and was strong in the eight-field rendering. In a seven-field comparison it remained strong at higher sampling resolution (mean $R^2=0.93$) and was weaker at the native resolution of a single eye model (0.44; Supplementary Fig. S2), which indicates that how finely the ring is sampled by the receptor lattice matters for composition.

A classical account of this illusion family is that a compressive nonlinearity applied to local motion-detector outputs before spatial pooling leaves a residual that does not cancel in an asymmetric pattern [@BachAtalaGerard2020RotatingSnakes]. An array of Reichardt detectors behaved accordingly: with linear pooling its output for static MIDD was near zero, and with the saturating nonlinearity it was directional, with the same net tangential direction as FlyVis for the two reference patterns and the same ordering of magnitudes (3MIDD larger than 4MIDD; Supplementary Fig. S4). Over a 21 × 21 grid of 4MIDD luminances, the directional components of the array and of one FlyVis network agreed in sign at 81% of grid points ($r=0.46$). A simple nonlinear detector array thus reproduces part of the structure of the FlyVis response, and the remainder shows that the trained network is not reducible to it.

### Sign and magnitude vary with phase and across networks
<!-- figure: 5 -->

Rotating the disk by a fraction of one sector period changes which receptors fall on which sectors. Across twelve such phases for $C=48$, networks kept their relative ordering (absolute-agreement ICC(A,1) = 0.76; Fig. 5A–B), and the value at the reference phase tracked the phase average ($r=0.88$; same sign in 39 of 50 networks). The magnitude nevertheless depended on phase: the phase-averaged mean was −0.0038 compared with −0.0093 at the reference phase, 34 networks changed sign at one or more phases, and the phase-averaged $A$ was negative in 32 of 50. Small shifts of the disk and restriction to the outer receptors, where the sectors are sampled most finely, changed $A$ and $S$ as well (Fig. 5C–E; Supplementary Figs. S5, S6). The coarse hexagonal lattice of the model eye, about 31 receptors across a disk of 72 sectors, is thus a major source of variation, and values at the reference phase (Figs. 1–3) should be read with this in mind.

Whether the Original and Mirror disks give flows of opposite sign depends on both components: the raw signs differ exactly when $|A|>|S|$. This was the case in 21 of 50 networks at $C=48$, 18 of 50 at $C=207$, and 12 of 50 for the 4MIDD (Fig. 1D; Fig. 3D). In the remaining networks the shared component was the larger one, and the direction-dependent signal rode on top of it.

Differences between networks were not explained by how well they solved the training task. Sintel endpoint error was weakly related to signed $A$ ($\rho=0.28$), to $|A|$ ($\rho=-0.22$), and to phase variability ($\rho=-0.33$); none of the three associations remained below 0.05 after Holm correction (adjusted $p=0.09$, $0.13$ and $0.06$). A link to the learned circuit solution is suggested by T4c tuning, which @LappalainenEtAl2024FlyVis showed to fall into distinct clusters across networks. Phase-averaged $A$ at $C=48$ was −0.0077 in the 31 networks with upward-selective T4c, as in the fly, and +0.0027 and +0.0026 in the 7 downward-selective and 12 untuned networks (permutation $p=0.018$, Holm-adjusted $p=0.053$; Fig. 5F; Supplementary Table S5A).

### Static structure in general evokes decoded motion

The response was not confined to the MIDD arrangement. When the sectors of each disk were shuffled while preserving its luminance histogram, the mirror-dependent component was as large as or larger than for the ordered disk (mean magnitude, ordered versus shuffled: 0.0106 versus 0.0133 at $C=48$, 0.0094 versus 0.0126 at $C=207$, and 0.0034 versus 0.0117 for the 4MIDD; Fig. 3F; Supplementary Fig. S5). Balanced disks, in which each transition occurs equally often in both directions (Fig. 3E), random-pixel images, and uniform gray fields also gave nonzero tangential flow. The responses to shuffled, balanced, and uniform images developed during training along with the MIDD response (Supplementary Fig. S8). Varying the number of sectors showed that the standard 72 and 96 sectors were not the optimum in any of ten networks; the response changed with spatial frequency and sampling (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Optic-flow training therefore makes the network and its decoder responsive to static spatial structure of many kinds. What distinguishes the ordered MIDD is not the size of its response but its regularity: a sign that follows the intermediate luminance across independently trained networks, and a four-level response that can be computed from three-level ones.

### The response depends on motion pathways and on the learned readout
<!-- figure: 6 -->

To ask which signals the decoded response relies on, we replaced the activity of selected cell types at the decoder input with their activity for a uniform gray field, leaving the recurrent network untouched (Fig. 6A–C; Supplementary Fig. S9). In ten networks, replacing T4, or T4 and T5 together, shifted the $C=48$ response by +0.054 (95% CI 0.014–0.100) and +0.067 (0.031–0.110) of the mean flow magnitude. The same replacement changed the decoded response to a rotating propeller and to moving edges. The static-pattern response thus passes through the same T4/T5 channels as real motion, in line with the dependence of the fly's behavioral response on these neurons [@AgrochaoEtAl2020IllusoryMotionFlies].

Finally, we paired each of ten networks with the decoder of every other (Fig. 6A, D–E; Supplementary Fig. S10). Matched pairs decoded both directions of a drifting grating correctly in 10 of 10 cases; mismatched pairs did so in 9 of 90, and their static response was larger (mean $|A|$ 0.028 versus 0.009 for matched pairs). Each decoder is thus adapted to its own network, and the decoded response to a static pattern is a property of the trained pair.

## Discussion

A network with the wiring of the fly visual system, trained only to estimate optic flow in rendered movies, acquired a direction-dependent response to static illusion patterns. The response was absent at initialization, appeared during training without any illusion pattern in the training data, took a sign set by the intermediate luminance in most independently trained networks, and combined across luminance levels by a difference rule. These are the properties that characterize the human percept of the same patterns [@KobayashiWatanabe2023MIDD], apart from polarity.

### Illusory motion as a by-product of learning to see motion

@WatanabeEtAl2018IllusoryMotion found that a network trained to predict video frames reproduced illusory rotation and proposed that the illusion follows from learning the regularities of real motion. The present result extends this observation along two dimensions. The objective is different, supervised flow estimation in place of frame prediction, and the architecture is a connectome with identified cell types in place of a generic convolutional hierarchy. That a static-pattern response appears in both cases suggests that it is a common consequence of fitting motion-sensitive circuitry to natural image sequences. The nonlinear detector array points to one way in which this can happen [@BachAtalaGerard2020RotatingSnakes]: a system tuned to natural scenes need not be linear, and a nonlinearity ahead of spatial pooling turns an asymmetric static pattern into a net directional signal. ON/OFF asymmetries of the kind that improve velocity estimation in natural scenes are a candidate source of such nonlinearity in the fly pathway [@FitzgeraldEtAl2011StimulusStatistics; @LeonhardtEtAl2016ONOFF].

### A shared rule with individual parameters

The human data separated two levels: local motion signals whose strength and reversal point differ between observers, and an integration stage that sums them in the same way in everyone [@KobayashiWatanabe2023MIDD]. The model shows the same separation. The three-level response curve was specific to each network, to the extent that another network's curve was a poor predictor, whereas the difference rule held in every network tested. In FlyVis the fifty networks share one connectome, so the individual part must come from the learned parameters. Fixing the wiring and the task does not fix the solution, as @LappalainenEtAl2024FlyVis showed for the tuning of T4c and as has been described for machine-learning models in general [@DAmourEtAl2022Underspecification]. The tendency of networks with fly-like T4c tuning to share one sign of the response suggests that this variation is structured by the circuit solution a network has found, a relationship that could be tested directly with the cell-type response properties of the fifty networks.

### What the fly model adds

Because every unit in FlyVis corresponds to a cell type, the response can be related to circuit elements. It depended on T4 and T5 activity at the decoder input, the neurons that carry the fly's behavioral response to static illusions [@AgrochaoEtAl2020IllusoryMotionFlies]. It also depended on the readout: decoders were not interchangeable between networks. In the fly, the corresponding readout is performed by lobula-plate tangential cells with fixed, wide receptive fields [@BorstEgelhaaf1989MotionDetection], and replacing the learned decoder with a tangential-cell-like pooling of T4/T5 activity would be a natural next step toward a comparison with behavior.

### Limitations

Decoded flow is a model output and we did not compare it quantitatively with fly behavior or with new human measurements; the comparison with human data concerns the form of the dependence on luminance and the summation rule. The response is small relative to that for physical motion, it is sensitive to the phase and placement of the disk on the coarse receptor lattice, and its polarity is opposite to the human percept. It is not specific to MIDD: other static images, including shuffled ones, evoke responses of similar or greater magnitude. The summation rule was established for the scalar $A$ in an eight-field rendering and was weaker at the native resolution of a single eye model. We did not train networks without the connectome constraint, so we cannot say which properties depend on it. The interventions replace activity at the decoder input in a model and are not equivalent to silencing neurons in an animal, and the detector array is a partial computational analogy.

## Methods

### FlyVis model and model cohorts

We used connectome-constrained recurrent FlyVis networks and their learned optical-flow decoders [@LappalainenEtAl2024FlyVis]. The reproducibility environment uses FlyVis v1.2.0, commit `92b3845cc426dd309a1a0e1b3890156c42e14021`. Official pretrained data are obtained with `flyvis download-pretrained`; `FLYVIS_ROOT_DIR` locates upstream models, results, and rendering caches separately from this repository's stimuli, precomputed analysis products, and custom checkpoints.

The official `flow/0000/000–049` ensemble supplies Figs. 1 and 3, the primary C=48 phase analysis in Fig. 5, expanded static/task-performance controls, and native S1 calibration. Fig. 4, Fig. 6 decoder-input replacement, and Supplementary Fig. S9 use experiment-specific ten-model subsets (000–009). Fig. 2 uses learning-custom seeds 000–013. Recurrent subtype interventions use established seeds 001, 002, 003, 010 (002 holdout) and comparators 000, 008 at step 250,000. Cross-swap uses official models 000, 005, 010, 015, 020, 025, 030, 035, 040, 045. Matching numeric IDs do not imply matching models across sets. Cohorts and analysis-specific measurement conditions are summarized in Table S1.

### MIDD stimuli and rendering

For the single-BoxEye stimuli, `src/flyvis_midd/stimuli.py` constructs 3MIDD Original sectors by repeating `[0,C,255]` 24 times and Mirror sectors by repeating `[255,C,0]`. The balanced negative repeats `[0,C,0,255,C,255]` 12 times. Each pattern therefore contains 72 equal-angle sectors. The 4MIDD Original repeats `[0,C1,255,C2]` 24 times; Mirror repeats `[0,C2,255,C1]`; the negative repeats `[0,C1,255,C2,0,C2,255,C1]` 12 times, yielding 96 sectors. Mirror denotes the specified change in spatial luminance order, not contrast inversion. The negative arrangements balance directed transitions and are analyzed as distinct stimuli.

The renderer uses 382×382-pixel images, inner/outer annulus diameters of 55/382 pixels, phase 0°, and white pixels outside the stimulus annulus. Integer luminances are divided by 255 before model input. The fixed native reference conditions are 3MIDD C=48 and C=207 and 4MIDD C1=85/C2=170. Fig. 3 uses the 15 C levels `16,32,48,64,80,96,112,128,143,159,175,191,207,223,239` plus that fixed 4MIDD condition.  Static movies repeat one image for 50 frames.

Single-BoxEye movies use `BoxEye(extent=15, kernel_size=13)` (721 receptors; shape `(50,1,721)`). Fig. 4 uses a centered `(3,2,3)` eight-field layout (decoded shape `(8,50,2,721)`) and luminances `48,80,112,143,175,191,207`; S9 activity uses the same layout, while replacement analyses include BoxEye and `highres_232`, with results kept rendering-specific. For Fig. 4, the image is centered on a black 3:2 canvas with horizontal padding and row offsets [0,1,0]. The high-resolution helper fits and bilinearly resizes the canvas (`align_corners=False`, rounded dimensions); placement is right-aligned at the layout's upper edge. Receptor values average 13×13 patches with zero padding. Global aggregation rounds (y,x) coordinates to eight decimals and averages coincident vectors per frame, negates v once, and averages tangent projection (dy/r,−dx/r) over unique positions and frames 10–49 within the 0.14–0.98 image-radius annulus centered on the placed-image bounds. This multi-field basis is opposite to the single-BoxEye clockwise basis (−dy/r,dx/r); eight-field calibration yields positive CCW and negative CW scalars in all ten models and does not automatically transfer to the seven-field S2 layout.

### Stimulus presentation and decoded optical flow

Static movies use 50 Hz (`dt=0.02 s`), 50 frames, and 1-s fade-in/state preparation. Figs. 1 and 3 use `run_flow_inference`: CPU float32 luminance, network/decoder `.eval()`, `torch.no_grad()`, `network.fade_in_state(1.0,dt,luminance[:,0])`, then `network.simulate(luminance,dt,initial_state=initial_state)` and decoder flow. Fade-in frames are excluded from the recorded movie. `analyze_midd_flow` measures canonicalized output at frames 10–49; `aggregate_midd_metrics` computes A/S/M and raw reversal, with negative responses separate. Expanded official controls and phase runs use the same inference; composition, learning, interventions, and cross-swap use their specified preparations and saved outputs.

Decoder flow is canonicalized to `(sample,frame,2,receptor)` with components `(u,v)`; the zero-based analysis window is frames 10–49. The flow fields in Fig. 1B–C are the decoder output averaged over these frames and over the 50 pretrained networks; for the static patterns the half-difference between the Original and Mirror fields is shown, and the mean tangential component of that field over the analysis ring equals A.

The S1 control uses static, clockwise, and counterclockwise three-blade propeller movies with 50 frames at 50 Hz and 128×128-pixel images. Direction agreement requires positive clockwise and negative counterclockwise `signed_tangent_ratio`. Rotation detection additionally requires the smaller moving-condition absolute ratio to exceed 0.1 and the smaller moving mean-flow magnitude divided by the static mean-flow magnitude to exceed 3.0. Direction agreement and detection are separate endpoints; propeller Static is not the MIDD negative condition.

### Tangential rotation and Original/Mirror decomposition

Main single-BoxEye analyses use `image_rotation_flow_metrics` in `src/flyvis_midd/rotation.py`. Receptor centers are in image coordinates, with x rightward and y downward. Decoder vectors are converted exactly once as `(u,v)→(u,−v)` before projection. For receptor displacement `(dx_i,dy_i)` from the mean receptor center and radius r_i, the clockwise tangent is `(-dy_i/r_i,dx_i/r_i)`. The analysis selects `0.14 r_max ≤ r_i ≤ 0.98 r_max`; the official single-BoxEye tables contain 698 selected receptors. `mean_signed_tangent`, denoted T, is the arithmetic mean of vector–tangent dot products over selected receptors and analyzed frames. Positive T denotes clockwise rotation. Values are decoded-flow units, not psychophysical velocities.

`signed_tangent_ratio` divides T by the mean Euclidean flow magnitude in the same window/annulus, using machine epsilon as a denominator floor. It is used for S1 detection and is not substituted for T in the main A/S analyses. Precomputed scalars already use the coordinate conversion defined for each experiment and are not flipped again. The multi-field global scalar and the activity/replacement analyses' image-radius ROI use their experiment-specific definitions rather than the single-BoxEye definition.

Within each model, condition, and checkpoint, we compute A=(T_O−T_M)/2, S=(T_O+T_M)/2, and M=|A|−|S|. Actual reversal uses the strict raw sign predicate T_O T_M<0, which is algebraically equivalent to M>0. Negative responses do not enter these definitions. Subtracting a common T_N preserves A but alters S and the raw reversal measure. Official-50 component distributions provide descriptive context in Supplementary Fig. S5; Fig. 5 combines the primary official-50 C=48 phase analysis with separate ten-model placement and radial analyses.

### Scalar composition and learning-dynamics analyses

For Fig. 4, A3(C) and observed A4 are Original/Mirror half-differences; the predictor is A3(C1)−A3(C2). Each model has an intercept OLS fit over 21 canonical C1<C2 pairs, with $R^2=1-\Sigma(y-\hat y)^2/\Sigma(y-\bar y)^2$. Identity residuals are observed A4 minus the unfitted predictor; RMSE is normalized by the A3 range. Metrics are averaged equally across models, not pooled across 210 pairs. Sign accuracy uses the unfitted predictor for evaluable pairs (18–21/model), defined by |A3(C1)−A3(C2)|/range(A3)≥0.05. This scalar analysis does not test full-vector additivity.

For Fig. 2, the checkpoint archive contains 2,586 files: 2,567 regular checkpoints, including initialization at step 0, and 19 irregular extras excluded from the trajectories. Regular support comprises step 0 and available 1,000-step intervals through each seed's endpoint. A/S/M are computed from paired Original/Mirror responses at each checkpoint. Operational onset is evaluated over fixed thresholds 0.0005, 0.001, 0.002, and 0.005 using both first crossing and the first of three consecutive regular checkpoints above threshold. Gaps are not bridged. Noncrossers are censored, and crossing medians explicitly describe crossers. Primary summaries use common steps 0–100,000. We additionally compute AUC and time to a fixed fraction of final |A| as retrospective, threshold-free descriptors. Normalized magnitude is |A|/G, with G the mean Original/Mirror Euclidean flow magnitude in the same annulus/window; zero denominators are flagged. At each step, medians and interquartile ranges use only seeds with data at that step; checkpoint observations are repeated measurements within seeds.

Fixed-evaluation Sintel EPE uses 15 samples from sequence IDs `0,5,10,15,22`, no augmentation or random temporal cropping, 19 source frames resampled/interpolated to 40 evaluated frames at `dt=0.02 s`, and a center-crop fraction of 0.7. This diagnostic evaluation is not held out and is distinct from training loss. The same regular checkpoint support is required for MIDD and EPE tables. Official-50 evaluation uses MultiTaskSintel with three vertical splits of original sequence IDs 0,5,10,15,22 (15 samples), all_frames=False and resampling/interpolation enabled. Model/decoder evaluation mode and no_grad are used after a 0.5-s steady-state preparation. Batches of four (last batch three) are weighted by sample count; EPE is the mean vector endpoint error over all 40 resampled frames and 721 receptors. Checkpoint hashes were verified against source model identities and canonical Original/Mirror responses checked before accepting EPE. The three official-50 EPE associations use A(C48, phase 0), its absolute value, and SD(A across phases)/mean paired G; 10,000 whole-model bootstrap intervals and Holm correction across these relations are reported.

### Cell activity and interventions

The decoder-input activity analysis examines activity from 34 decoder-input cell types in ten models. Moving-edge calibration specifies 12 directions at 30° intervals, intensities 0 and 1, speed 19, `dt=0.005 s`, offsets `[-10,11]`, height 80, and 1 s pre/post periods. The primary preferred direction is leave-one-model-out (LOMO). MIDD cell evidence is the projected Original/Mirror half-difference, normalized and screened for identifiability as defined below. Signed and absolute Spearman coupling are evaluated within models across the seven C levels. Supplementary Fig. S9 shows these identifiable activity entries; available model counts vary by subtype.

Decoder-input replacement substitutes a frame-matched baseline for targeted channels without re-simulating the recurrent network. The corrected-family endpoint is normalized loss $L=1-$gain; T4a is primary, T4b exploratory, and T4/T5 groups are defined separately. No-op and nontarget-invariance checks assess specificity; alternate estimates are not pooled. For the decoder-input activity analysis, raw evidence e(C) is the space/time mean of the Original/Mirror activity half-difference multiplied by the LOMO preferred-direction tangent weight within the common annulus. Across seven C levels, R=sqrt(mean_C e(C)²), normalized evidence is e(C)/(R+10⁻⁸), and identifiability requires R>max_C|e_negative(C)|+10⁻⁸. For the decoder-input replacement analysis, the baseline averages intact activity over all 21 3MIDD conditions (seven luminances × Original/Mirror/negative), preserving frame, spatial sample, cell channel, and receptor position. Only targeted channels are replaced. Given intact and intervention seven-luminance A3 vectors x and y, gain=(y·x)/(x·x), without an intercept; loss=1−gain and shape residual=||y−gain x||/||x||. Gain is undefined if x·x is nonfinite or ≤10⁻¹². Bootstrap intervals are computed by resampling model losses with replacement 50,000 times, using random seed 10012 and percentile 2.5/97.5 intervals for the mean. The one-sided sign-flip test enumerates all 2ⁿ sign assignments and uses p=(1+number of permuted means≥observed mean)/(2ⁿ+1). T4a baseline is a singleton confirmatory family (q=p); TmY14, T2a, and Tm16 baseline form the secondary family; group interventions form a separate family; remaining single-type baseline interventions form the exploratory screen. Other interventions retain their catalog families. Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment is applied separately within each nonsingleton family. Separately assigned targets are not included in the exploratory family's multiple-comparison correction.

Recurrent interventions neutralize T4c/T4d subtypes and compare matched-random node sets in the same instances (199 draws). MIDD summaries average twelve phases (0°–13.75°, 1.25° steps); paired Sintel outcomes are intervention-minus-intact EPE. The state hook zeros selected activity during MIDD fade-in/simulation and evaluated Sintel frames (after 0.5-s gray preparation). Each random set samples 721 T4 nodes without replacement within twelve sectors, matching target subtype counts; all T4 subtypes, including the target, are eligible. Seed/target/draw sets are reused across phases and Sintel samples. Sintel uses evaluation-role rows of `part4_sintel_scene_disjoint_split.csv` (indices 36,39,40,41,48,49,50; original sequences 12,13,16), excluding calibration sequences 2,7,9. MultiTaskSintel uses three vertical splits, 19 frames, 0.7 center crop, `dt=0.02 s`, resampling/interpolation, and no augmentation or random temporal crop. EPE differences are averaged across splits within sequence, then equally across sequences per seed; random controls retain draw identity. These are model perturbations, not biological knockouts.

Cross-swap is a descriptive 10×10 network–decoder design for 3MIDD C=48 (1-s gray preparation; 50 frames; stable window 10–49). Matched pairs share IDs; each network has nine mismatched decoders. A fixed balanced two-way centered sum-of-squares decomposition assigns row-mean and column-mean deviations to network and decoder, with residual $E_{ij}=Y_{ij}-\bar Y_{i\cdot}-\bar Y_{\cdot j}+\bar Y$; components sum to total centered SS. MIDD A and physical-grating $Q=(\bar u_{\mathrm{right}}-\bar u_{\mathrm{left}})/2$ are analyzed separately; early/late windows and |Q| are sensitivity endpoints. Ten thousand joint-identity bootstrap draws preserve matched cells. Intervals are conditional robustness summaries, not causal variance components; with one observation per pair, residual and measurement error cannot be separated. Within-column row and within-row column permutations are alignment diagnostics. Gratings use three spatial cycles, one temporal cycle/s, contrast 0.35, 50 frames at `dt=0.02 s` after 1-s gray preparation. Correct directions require mean rightward u>0 and leftward u<0 over the original grating receptors at frames 10–49. Both-direction accuracy differs from Q>0; original Q is checked first. Nine mismatches are summarized within network before matched/mismatched comparisons.

### Spatial and matched-static controls

Matched-static controls use all 50 official models and ten histogram-preserving sector shuffles per family (seed 2026100301). Luminance is permuted within equal raster-area classes, preserving sector and image histograms. Canonical and shuffled magnitudes use the same image/full-LR half-difference; shuffles are averaged within model, with ten-draw resolution for ranks/percentiles. Mean/median differences and 10,000 whole-model percentile intervals use seed 2026100504. Other controls are histogram-matched random pixels, uniform gray 128, and 382×382 static/CW/CCW propellers. Operators remain distinct: Original/Mirror, image/LR, CW/CCW, and singleton uniform T/G.

The primary phase analysis tests C=48 in all 50 official models at twelve equally spaced phases j/12 of the 15° color-unit period. C=207/4MIDD phase tests, placement, and radial profiles use official models 000–009. These phases span half the balanced negative's 30° period. ICC(A,1) is the absolute-agreement single-measure intraclass correlation; it estimates the share of model-by-phase variance attributable to between-model differences, with phase and residual variance contributing to the remainder. ICC(A,1) and consistency ICC use the model×phase matrix; 10,000 whole-model bootstrap intervals are conditional on this grid. Phase-wise reversal and reversal of phase-averaged raw pairs are distinct endpoints. Fixed ±3-pixel translations use white fill, no interpolation/wrapping, and a fixed retinal annulus. Pixel hashes identify exchanged/duplicate reflection/rotation stimuli; their sign changes are not independent network evidence.

Radial geometry uses actual BoxEye receptor positions. Euclidean nearest-neighbor distances determine local spacing; unit-period arc is r×2π/24. Seven equal radial bins span 0.14–0.98 r_max. T_O,T_M,A,S,G and reversal are recomputed from saved flow within each bin and the outer subset satisfying period arc≥2×local spacing. Receptors are equally weighted within each ROI and analyzed frames 10–49. Full-annulus recalculation is checked against source scalars before ROI comparisons. The geometric criterion does not ensure sharp-edge sampling or isolate aliasing; these comparisons do not establish resolution invariance.

Sector counts were 36,48,60,72,84,96,120 for 3MIDD and 32,48,64,72,80,96,120 for 4MIDD, at phase fractions 0 and 0.5 of one color unit. Counts maintain integer complete units and balanced negatives. The count cohort was the fixed pilot 008/009/005 followed by 000–004/006/007 (N=10). Image dimensions, annulus, and luminances were unchanged; edge density covaries with sector count. Within-model normalized response contrasts and Spearman summaries are bootstrapped across models, never across sectors.

Conditional leave-one-luminance-out fits exclude all A4 pairs containing the held luminance; independently measured A3 values remain available to form predictions. Each of 21 pairs is predicted in two folds, giving 42 repeated predictions per model. Ordinary least-squares slope/intercept are fitted on training pairs only. R², RMSE, and sign accuracy are summarized within model and then equally across models. The sign mask is fixed to the canonical predictor's 5% A3-range rule; affine CV sign accuracy differs from unfitted component sign agreement. Leave-one-pair-out is secondary.

Secondary metrics include RMSE, A3-range-normalized RMSE and sign accuracy.

Additional comparison predictors comprise the A3 sum, assignment permutations, and random antisymmetric potentials B(C1)−B(C2). On this complete grid, A3-value and luminance-label permutations are the same null. Smooth Gaussian-process potentials use squared-exponential lengthscale 0.25 as primary and 0.1/0.5 as sensitivities, centered and RMS-matched within model. We use 2,000 shared null draws and 10,000 model-bootstrap draws, seed 2026100305, with Holm adjustment of four ensemble null-rank comparisons. Rank p is (1+number of null draws at least as extreme)/(2000+1); the minimum is 1/2001 and the four-comparison Holm floor is 4/2001.

### Additional biological and circuit constraints

Saved T4c phenotype labels were compared with phase-averaged 3MIDD C=48 responses in the official 50-model set. Mean signed $A$ was −0.007707 for upward-selective (n=31), +0.002674 for downward-selective (n=7), and +0.002613 for non-direction-tuned (n=12) models. The cluster comparison was suggestive but inconclusive after correction (eta-squared=0.157, permutation p=.0176, Holm p=.0528); phase-instability and sign-change comparisons were also inconclusive (Holm p=.684 and .301; Table S5A). No independent broader biological-response score was available, so no composite fidelity measure was constructed. This association does not establish T4c causation or general prediction of MIDD output.

In the official 50-model cohort, the fixed equal-weight T4/T5 activity projection did not establish a common directional readout or model-level association with decoded $A$: the mean activity component was +0.000141 (95% CI [−0.000677, +0.001055], Holm p=1.000), its association with decoded $A$ was Spearman rho=.116 (95% CI [−.193, .415], Holm p=1.000), and sign agreement was 27/50 (54%; Table S5B). This is inconclusive for the tested fixed aggregate readout and is not evidence that T4/T5 activity contains no MIDD-related signal.

A separate broad ON-minus-OFF source-current summary was likewise inconclusive: mean $I_{ONOFF}$ was 4.52×10⁻⁸ (95% CI [−1.12×10⁻⁷, 2.16×10⁻⁷], Holm p=1.000), its association with decoded $A$ was Spearman rho=.065 (95% CI [−.216, .341], Holm p=1.000), and descriptive sign agreement was 26/50 (52%; Table S5B). A secondary OFF_A association with decoded $A$ was negative (Spearman rho=−.357, Holm-adjusted p=.048 within the secondary family), but remains a secondary observational result and does not identify a pathway mechanism. No D2 causal intervention was performed.
